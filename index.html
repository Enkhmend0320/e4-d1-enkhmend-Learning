Learning

My Journey
from the Gobi

What learning means to the son of a camel-herding family

B. Enkhmend · UFE Tech AI Engineer, 1st Year · English Independent Assignment Instructor: Suzanna Oktai
01
Good morning, everyone. My name is B. Enkhmend. I am a first-year AI Engineering student at UFE Tech. I come from a camel-herding family in the Gobi Desert. When many people hear the word “learning,” they imagine a classroom, a teacher, and a textbook. My experience is wider than that. I learn at school, but I also learn from my family, our camels, and the land around us. Today I would like to share what learning means to me, the challenges I face, and how education can help me build my future while staying connected to my roots.
My learning environment

My classroom is larger than a school

I learn through lessons, daily work, and close attention to the land around me.

Home School The Gobi
02
For me, the classroom is much larger than a school building. At home, I learn by watching experienced people and helping with real work. At school, I learn ideas that help me understand the world beyond my local area. The Gobi is also a teacher. Its weather can change quickly, so I must pay attention. Every place gives me a different kind of knowledge. Together, home, school, and nature have taught me to be curious, careful, and responsible.
Knowledge at home

My first teachers

  • My family teaches me to care for animals and finish every task.
  • The weather teaches me to prepare before conditions change.
  • Camels teach me patience because trust grows slowly.
03
My family members were my first teachers. When I was younger, I watched them care for the camels and make decisions about the herd. They taught me that responsibility means finishing a task even when the weather is difficult. The land taught me to prepare before conditions change. The camels taught me patience. A camel does not trust a person immediately, so I must stay calm and act with care. These lessons are practical, but they also shape my character.
Home and school

Two kinds of knowledge

The strongest learning happens when I connect experience with new ideas.

From home

I read tracks, weather, and animal behaviour.

From school

I study languages, science, and the wider world.

04
I receive two kinds of knowledge, and I need both of them. At home, I learn to read tracks, notice changes in animal behaviour, and understand signs in the weather. At school, I study languages and science, and I discover how people live in other places. These kinds of knowledge do not compete with each other. They support each other. For example, science can explain something I have already seen in nature. My daily experience also helps me remember ideas from class.

05
A typical day moves between family duties and schoolwork. Early in the morning, I may help check the herd. This teaches me to observe small changes. During school hours, I study new ideas and ask questions. In the evening, I help my family again, and real work develops my judgement. Later, when the day becomes quiet, I practise English. I do not always have a long time to study, but short and regular practice can still create steady progress.
Challenges

Learning takes determination

Distance and weather can change my plans, especially in winter.

Internet access is sometimes slow, so I save materials when the signal is better.

Time and energy are limited after herding work, so I study in focused sessions.

06
Learning in the Gobi brings real challenges. Distance and severe weather can change travel plans, especially in winter. Internet access can also be slow or unstable. When the signal is good, I try to save the materials that I will need later. Time is another challenge because family work can leave me tired. I cannot control every condition, but I can control my response. I prepare in advance, study in focused sessions, and continue one step at a time.
My method

How I keep learning

I make progress when I stay curious and use knowledge in real situations.

01

02

03

Practise

04

Reflect

I think about what worked and what I can improve.

07
I use a simple method to keep learning. First, I observe. I look closely before I decide what something means. Next, I ask questions because a clear question can save time and prevent mistakes. Then I practise. Reading an answer once is not enough, so I use the skill again until I feel confident. Finally, I reflect. I think about what worked and what I should change next time. This method helps me both in school and while working with camels.

08
English is important to me because it opens a wider world. It gives me access to more books, videos, and online lessons. It also helps me communicate with people from different countries. However, English is not only a way to receive information. It is also a way to share my own story. I can explain life in the Gobi, introduce our camel-herding culture, and help other people understand its value. I want knowledge and communication to move in both directions.
My conclusion

My roots give me direction.
Learning gives me choices.

I want to grow without forgetting where I come from.

Thank you

09
To conclude, my roots in the Gobi give me direction. They teach me where I come from and what I value. Learning gives me choices. It helps me understand my home more deeply and prepares me for opportunities beyond it. I do not want education to separate me from my culture. I want it to help me protect what is valuable, solve new problems, and choose my own future. I hope my story shows that learning can happen anywhere. Thank you very much for listening.
